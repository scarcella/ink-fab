## Starting with headcount

**Barristers (England & Wales):** There are 17,864 practising barristers in England and Wales, of which approximately 3,000 are employed barristers. The self-employed Bar — roughly 14,800 — is overwhelmingly contentious. Criminal, civil, public law, employment, commercial, Chancery, family — almost all of it involves court or tribunal work. Even the non-litigious Chancery and tax barristers draft opinions and advise on disputes. Conservatively, 85%+ of the self-employed Bar does work that involves case law research as a core activity. That gives us roughly **~12,500–13,000 barristers** as potential users.

**Scottish advocates:** Around 460, nearly all contentious. Add them.

**Northern Ireland barristers:** Around 600, predominantly court-based.

**Litigation solicitors (England & Wales):** This is where it gets harder. There are currently 174,287 practising solicitors in England and Wales, but the majority are not litigators. Among the Lawyer UK 100 firms, Dispute Resolution & Litigation accounts for 2,438 lawyers — but that's just the top 100 firms. Looking across the profession, litigation and dispute resolution is typically estimated at around 15–20% of the solicitor population (including personal injury, clinical negligence, employment, commercial litigation, criminal, public law, insurance litigation, and general civil litigation). That gives a range of roughly **26,000–35,000 litigation solicitors**.

**Employment tribunal specialists, regulatory litigators, and in-house litigators** overlap with the above but add some further reach.

**Judicial assistants and judges:** There were approximately 32,500 judges and barristers working in the UK as of mid-2024. Stripping out the barristers already counted, and recognising that this includes lay magistrates, there are roughly 2,000–3,000 professional judges plus their judicial assistants/clerks who do case law research daily.

## The honest caveats

**This is a UK-only number.** The product proposition — primary law, case-law-intensive, contentious work — translates directly to other common law jurisdictions: Australia, Ireland, Singapore, Hong Kong, Canada, and parts of the US. Each of those multiplies the TAM significantly. Australia alone has a Bar and litigation solicitor base comparable in density to the UK's.

**Not all 50,000 are equal.** The barristers' chambers segment is high-value but low-volume — roughly 300 sets of chambers buying collectively for their members. The litigation solicitor segment is higher-volume but has more price sensitivity, especially at high-street level. The Magic Circle and Silver Circle litigation departments are small in headcount but have the highest willingness to pay.

**You're competing for budget, not just attention.** Most litigation firms already spend £3,000–£10,000+ per fee earner on Westlaw and/or Lexis. Your product either needs to displace a portion of that spend (hard) or sit alongside it as complementary tooling (easier to sell but you're adding to an already heavy technology budget). The strongest positioning is as a product that does something Westlaw and Lexis *can't* do — the case analysis, authority mapping, and skeleton drafting layer — rather than trying to replace their database.

**The research tool TAM understates the workflow tool TAM.** If you build the drafting features (skeleton arguments, grounds of appeal, case analysis memos), you're no longer just a research product — you're a productivity tool, which commands higher willingness to pay and broader adoption. That could push the per-seat price and the addressable user base upward.
